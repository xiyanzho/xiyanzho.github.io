Goal-Conditioned Contrastive Reinforcement Learning in Discrete Action Spaces

Research Assistant · advised by Kellen Kanarios · Jan 2026 – Present

Goal-conditioned RL asks for a single policy π(a | s, g) that can reach any goal g, learned from unlabeled experience with no reward signal beyond "did you arrive." Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) approaches this without temporal-difference learning at all: instead of bootstrapping a value function, it trains a critic by classification, so that f(s, a, g) estimates the density ratio of the discounted future-state occupancy — how much more likely g is to occur later given action a in state s, relative to a random goal. The critic becomes a representation-learning objective rather than a bootstrapped one, which removes target networks and the usual sources of TD instability.

This project reimplements that method in JAX and adapts it to discrete action spaces, with the long-horizon crafting environment Craftax as the eventual target. My role is implementation and experiment design; Kellen sets algorithmic direction.

method

The critic is bilinear, f(s, a, g) = φ(s, a) · ψ(g) / √d, with φ and ψ as ensembled MLP encoders and a choice of energy function (negative L2, dot product, or cosine). Positives are drawn by sampling, for each timestep, a future goal from the same trajectory under geometric weighting γΔt — the discounted-occupancy sampler. Negatives are the other goals in the batch, giving a B×B logit matrix whose diagonal holds the positives, trained under InfoNCE and related binary-classification variants. Stability comes from the logsumexp penalty of the original paper, projection of the encoders onto a sphere of radius √d, and masking of false negatives where two batch elements happen to share a goal.

For continuous control the actor is standard SAC. The discrete case is the substantive change: the actor is trained through a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation with an annealed temperature and an entropy coefficient adapted to a target fraction of log|A|. Actor goals are sampled uniformly from the batch rather than from the future of the current trajectory, since future-only goals would train the policy to reach only states it already visits. The whole training loop is jitted end-to-end over vmapped environments, with a wrapper stack supplying goals, trajectory ids, and autoreset across the brax, navix, gymnax, and craftax suites alongside my own environments.

environments

Rather than moving straight to Craftax, I built a sequence of grid worlds that each isolate a single hypothesis about what the contrastive objective can represent.

what worked

A purely bilinear contrastive critic, with no forward model and no auxiliary losses, solves discrete navigation (0.85 against a 0.06 random baseline, on a grid an oracle solves 99.8% of the time) and transfers to the Navix benchmark at 0.92 against 0.21.

The more interesting result is the inventory environment, where goals are abstract and many-to-one: a goal specifies a resource count, not a location, so a great many states satisfy it. At 0.68 success the obvious worry is that the agent has simply learned to collect everything, so I ran three checks against that. Success degrades gracefully with goal difficulty rather than falling off a cliff (0.97 / 0.70 / 0.46 across easy, medium and hard goal buckets). The final inventory is roughly nine times richer in the commanded resource than in the others (4.76 against 0.53). And a policy trained only on static goals generalizes zero-shot to a moving target — a goal recomputed every step as "one more than you currently hold" — sustaining collection to the environment cap, a distribution it never saw in training.

a metric that lies

A finding that recurs across every environment: the contrastive training metric is decoupled from control quality, and at times inverted.

The reason is structural. Contrastive accuracy measures whether the true future state outranks other states in the batch, which a critic that ignores its action argument entirely can do perfectly well. Control requires the opposite comparison: ranking actions against each other at a fixed state and goal. InfoNCE never places two same-state, different-action pairs in contrast, so nothing in the loss directly pressures the representation to be action-discriminative. The objective is measuring one axis while evaluation depends on another.

This is why the diagnostics I found most useful were the ones that bypass the training loss altogether — rolling out argmaxa f(s, a, g) directly to attribute failure to the critic rather than the actor, and consistency probes that check whether the data reaching the critic means what the loss assumes it means.

where it breaks

Matthews et al., Goal-Conditioned Agents that Learn Everything All at Once (ICML 2026), argue that in procedurally generated worlds state-based goals are meaningless, and that methods assuming a functional state-to-goal mapping — contrastive RL named explicitly — "cannot natively handle" the setting.

I converted the inventory environment into exactly that regime. Resources deplete on collection and respawn elsewhere, the layout is redrawn every episode, and the agent must read a sparse resource map from its observation instead of memorizing where things are. The algorithm, the goal space, and the code are unchanged; only layout randomization was added. Success collapses from 0.68 to 0.08, medium and hard goals fall to zero, and selectivity inverts — the agent ends up holding more of the resources it was not asked for. The critic, meanwhile, keeps training happily.

The tension worth pursuing is that this is not quite the predicted failure. The same method already succeeds on abstract, many-to-one, position-invariant inventory goals, which is close to the case the paper says should not work; what breaks it is procedural randomization specifically. Candidate mechanisms I can distinguish experimentally: the sparse resource map being washed out by normalization; the goal encoder being inventory-only, so that all spatial reasoning is forced into the state encoder; and uniform goal sampling for the actor being too hard a curriculum in this regime. The key-and-door environment is the follow-up diagnostic, separating "cannot handle procedural generation" from "cannot represent causal, multi-step plans" — though it carries a confound I have yet to resolve, since aliasing every state to a two-dimensional goal makes most in-batch negatives secretly positive.

status and limitations

Stated plainly: results so far are single-seed and CPU-only, without error bars or external baselines run in the same harness, so the 0.68-to-0.08 comparison is a controlled one-variable manipulation but not yet a statistically supported claim. The key-and-door environment is built and smoke-tested but has not been trained. The repository is named for a planned hierarchical extension toward Craftax that has not been started — making flat contrastive RL correct on discrete actions, and characterizing where it fails, turned out to be a substantial project on its own.
