Goal-Conditioned Contrastive Reinforcement Learning in Discrete Action Spaces

Research Assistant · advised by Kellen Kanarios · Jan 2026 – Present

Goal-conditioned RL asks for a single policy π(a | s, g) that can reach any goal g, learned from unlabeled experience with no reward signal beyond "did you arrive." Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) approaches this without temporal-difference learning at all: instead of bootstrapping a value function, it trains a critic by classification, so that f(s, a, g) estimates the density ratio of the discounted future-state occupancy — how much more likely g is to occur later given action a in state s, relative to a random goal.

This project builds on a JAX implementation of that method for discrete action spaces, written by Kellen Kanarios, and asks why it fails on some discrete goal-conditioned tasks. The answer we arrived at is one measurable mechanism: a goal-conditioned critic stops discriminating between actions when the goal admits more than one satisfying state. Kellen wrote the original implementation, including the critic, InfoNCE training loop and a Gumbel-Softmax actor, and sets the algorithmic direction. I built the environments, goal encodings and baseline, debugged and extended the agent, and designed and ran the experiments.

method

The critic is bilinear, f(s, a, g) = φ(s, a) · ψ(g) / √d, with φ and ψ as ensembled MLP encoders. Positives are drawn by sampling, for each timestep, a future goal from the same trajectory under geometric weighting γΔt; negatives are the other goals in the batch, giving a B×B logit matrix trained under InfoNCE. For discrete actions the actor is trained on the expected critic value over all actions under its softmax policy, with a learned entropy coefficient; Gumbel-Softmax sampling is used for data collection. The whole training loop is jitted end-to-end over vmapped environments.

Rather than working on someone else's benchmark, I built small grid worlds (8×8 navigation and foraging, 6×6 key–door) in which goal structure is a controllable variable: a navigation task, a foraging task with wood / water / diamond resources and count goals ("hold one wood"), and a key–door task. As a matched non-contrastive baseline I built a goal-conditioned Double-DQN with hindsight replay on rlax, sharing the environments, buffer, batch size, horizons and evaluation.

the finding

Some goals are satisfied by exactly one world state ("stand on this cell"); others by several ("hold one wood" when two wood tiles exist). Performance degrades as the number of satisfiers grows — and the degradation lives in the critic, not the policy.

To show that directly rather than infer it from returns, I use a probe I call critic lift: over 65,536 states, how often the critic's greedy action is the one that actually moves toward the correct target, above what the critic's own action bias would produce by chance. Across 15 foraging runs, lift separates cleanly by satisfier count — the lowest one-satisfier run (0.139) sits above the highest two-satisfier run (0.076) — while the one-satisfier configurations span 100% to 80% of achievable performance. Lift does not fall as the task gets harder; it falls when a second satisfier appears.

the isolating control

Adding a tile makes the board busier and can make the goal ambiguous. To separate the two, I hold the environment, agent and added tile fixed and change only the goal's form. A count goal gains a second satisfier when a second wood tile appears; a positional goal stays unique however many tiles exist.

not unique to contrastive learning

The Double-DQN baseline degrades by a comparable factor on the same manipulation (×0.64, against ×0.88 for contrastive RL). So this is a property of goal-conditioned value learning rather than an artifact of the contrastive objective — a broader claim, and a narrower one than the project set out to make. It relates to Matthews et al. (Goal-Conditioned Agents that Learn Everything All at Once, ICML 2026), who argue that methods assuming a functional state–goal mapping, such as contrastive RL, cannot natively handle goals that many states satisfy. Our results suggest one measurable mechanism behind such difficulty: it is graded rather than categorical, and it also affects a TD baseline.

correcting ourselves

An earlier version of these notes reported that layout randomization collapsed foraging success from 0.68 to 0.08. That result did not survive. A seven-run configuration screen found that six of seven single-knob changes beat the published configuration, and one — the direction of the InfoNCE loss (forward rather than backward) — took the headline task from 0.342 to 0.816. Earlier ablations had been measuring a bad configuration.

Re-running the full ladder, key–door and the positional control on the corrected configuration withdrew three claims: that contrastive RL falls below its own random floor (it reaches 65% of achievable), that an untuned baseline beats it threefold (0.977 against 0.818, and the tuned method wins on the two-satisfier arm), and that a single statistic orders both environments monotonically (what holds is a within-environment separation). The satisfier effect itself survived all three corrections, in a cleaner form. The broader lesson: ablation ladders built on an untuned configuration can inflate an effect roughly threefold.

Along the way a long list of explanations was proposed, tested and ruled out — false-negative contamination, goal aliasing, causal chain depth, respawn randomization, board density, centroid averaging, and critic goal-blindness among them. Several early failures were self-inflicted: a replay buffer storing discrete actions as [a, 0, 0] rather than one-hot, and a task in which 57% of commanded goals were unreachable.

a metric that lies

A finding that recurs across environments: the contrastive training metric is decoupled from control quality, and at times inverted. On navigation, an action-blind critic scored 0.55 contrastive accuracy and 0.03 success; a correct one scored 0.08 and 0.85. Contrastive accuracy asks whether the true future state outranks other states in the batch, which a critic ignoring its action argument can do perfectly well. Control requires ranking actions against each other at a fixed state and goal, and InfoNCE never places two same-state, different-action pairs in contrast. This is why the diagnostics I found most useful bypass the training loss entirely — rolling out argmaxa f(s, a, g) directly, and probes like critic lift.

status and limitations

About 120 completed runs at 20M steps, every headline claim at three or more seeds and reproducible from logs by one script. The aim is a mechanism-level result: it names a variable (satisfier multiplicity), isolates it with a control, measures it inside the critic, and replicates it in a second environment and a second algorithm family.

Stated plainly: all results come from one critic family on small grid worlds (8×8 foraging, 6×6 key–door), so every claim is a grid-world claim until tested more broadly. Tuning is asymmetric — contrastive RL received a seven-configuration screen, the baseline one knob on one arm. Two ablation rungs are still on the old configuration, and a centroid-averaging signal reappeared on a single seed at three doors after being refuted twice; both are open.
